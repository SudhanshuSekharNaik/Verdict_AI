# Landmark Precedents in Indian Criminal Jurisprudence

### K.M. Nanavati v. State of Maharashtra, AIR 1962 SC 605
**Bench & Court:** Supreme Court of India (Subba Rao, J.)
**Core Holding:** Established the definitive test for "grave and sudden provocation" under Exception 1. The Court held that while a wife's confession of adultery constitutes grave provocation, the accused did not commit the fatal act in immediate reaction. Dropping his family at a cinema, driving to the naval dockyard, obtaining a revolver and cartridges under a false pretext, and proceeding to the victim's residence constituted a cooling-off interval with intermediate calculated steps that negated the element of suddenness as a matter of law.

### Sharad Birdhichand Sarda v. State of Maharashtra, (1984) 4 SCC 116
**Bench & Court:** Supreme Court of India (Fazal Ali, J.)
**Core Holding:** Formulated the five golden principles (Panchsheel) governing circumstantial evidence in criminal trials: (1) Circumstances from which the conclusion of guilt is drawn must be fully established; (2) Facts must be consistent only with the hypothesis of guilt; (3) Circumstances must be of a conclusive nature; (4) They must exclude every possible hypothesis except the guilt of the accused; (5) The chain of evidence must be complete without leaving any reasonable ground for innocence.

### Babu v. State of Kerala, (2010) 9 SCC 189
**Bench & Court:** Supreme Court of India
**Core Holding:** Reaffirmed the bedrock principle that suspicion, however strong or grave, cannot take the place of legal proof beyond reasonable doubt. The prosecution must establish an unbroken causal nexus between the accused and the alleged criminal act.

### State of U.P. v. Deoman Upadhyaya, AIR 1960 SC 1125
**Bench & Court:** Supreme Court of India
**Core Holding:** Clarified the admissibility of information leading to the discovery of a distinct physical fact under evidence law. The information must relate distinctly to the fact discovered in police custody to be admissible.

### Akhtar v. State (NCT of Delhi), (2006) 10 SCC 473
**Bench & Court:** Supreme Court of India
**Core Holding:** Re-emphasized that where an accused seeks the benefit of sudden provocation, the absence of prior premeditation and the immediacy of the retaliation are indispensable. Any conscious search for a lethal weapon after the provocation breaks the suddenness defense.
